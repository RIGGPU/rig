Receipts

The meter is public.

Every output and every rental writes a receipt. Each hour the receipts are hashed into one root, and the root goes into a Robinhood Chain transaction anyone can read.

How to check one
1Open a receipt and read its fields: what ran, for how long, what it cost.
2Hash the fields. That is the leaf.
3Hash it with each step of its proof. You arrive at the root of its hour.
4Compare with the transaction onchain. Your browser does all four in the app.

$RIG

Hold it for free outputs. Spend it and it burns.

The token has two jobs, and the ledger enforces both.

Hold

1,000 $RIG makes outputs free

A wallet holding at least 1,000 $RIG makes 50 images and 2 five-second videos free every UTC day in the app. New ones arrive at 00:00 UTC, and a free output that fails does not count.

Spend

Pay in $RIG and it burns

Credits bought with $RIG are spent before any others. Each hour, the tokens behind the credits that were used are sent to the dead address. Unused ones come back to you as the same tokens.

$RIG launches on Robinhood Chain. The contract address appears here the moment it is live.
FAQ

Straight answers.

The docs go through every step with the exact requests.

What is RIG?

RIG is a GPU cloud you pay for on Robinhood Chain. It rents whole NVIDIA GPUs by the second with SSH and Jupyter, and it runs ready media models that make images, video and voice for a fixed price per output. Both are paid from one balance of RIG credits.

What is a RIG credit and what does it cost?

One credit pays for one dollar of outputs and rentals at their listed prices. At launch a credit costs 0.70 USDG, so everything is 30% under its listed price. Balances are cut down to the last micro-credit and never rounded up. Unused credits refund to the wallet that bought them at the price it paid, in the asset it paid with.

How do I pay?

Connect a wallet such as MetaMask or Rabby and sign one message to sign in. It costs no gas and moves no funds. Then buy credits in Billing with USDG, ETH or $RIG, from 5 to 1,000 dollars at a time. Your wallet switches to Robinhood Chain, the transfer goes to the RIG treasury, and the credits show up when the chain confirms it.

What comes with a rental?

A whole GPU instance with a 50 GB disk, PyTorch and CUDA installed, SSH on its own port and Jupyter behind an SSH tunnel. Add a public SSH key, pick a GPU or let rig/auto pick the cheapest that fits, and connect. Billing starts the second SSH is ready, so the start is free, and it stops the second you end the instance. A wallet runs one instance at a time.

What if a GPU fails to start, or my credits run out?

A GPU that never starts is never billed. RIG moves the rental once to the next cheapest GPU with at least the same memory, and if that fails too it stops and tells you why. While an instance runs, its cost comes out second by second, and when the balance can no longer pay for the next second the instance ends. The app warns ten minutes ahead.

How does an agent use RIG?

Two ways. With an API key, the agent connects to the MCP server at /mcp and gets 14 tools: balance, models, outputs, GPUs, instances, SSH keys and receipts. Every key can carry a spend cap per UTC day and can be stopped with one switch that only your wallet controls. Without any key, the agent pays each output with x402: it signs one USDG transfer (EIP-3009), RIG submits it and pays the gas.

What exactly is written onchain?

Deposits are ordinary transfers to the treasury on Robinhood Chain. An x402 payment is a signed USDG transfer that the treasury submits. Every hour the receipts of that hour are hashed into a Merkle root, and the root is written into the data of a transaction. A burn of $RIG is a transfer to the dead address. Each is one transaction you can open in the explorer.

Where do the GPUs come from?

From data-center and community capacity reached through Runpod. RIG reads the live catalog, lists each GPU at one rate per hour, and starts your instance on the cheapest machine of the type you asked for. Outputs run on hosted model endpoints from the same provider.

Is the code open?

Yes. The server is one Node.js program with no dependencies: the ledger, the chain code with its own keccak-256 and secp256k1, the provider adapter, the API, the MCP server and the x402 flow. The test suite runs 78 end-to-end checks against a real server process.
